# Initial Problem Hypothesis — AI Sales Agent

**Date:** 24 October 2026

## 1\. Target Customer

Small and medium-sized coaching institutes and training centres that receive enquiries through WhatsApp, phone calls, website forms, and social media.

## 2\. Problem Hypothesis

We hypothesise that some coaching institutes lose potential student bookings because enquiries are not followed up consistently, responses are delayed, and staff manage enquiries manually across multiple channels.

This is a hypothesis, not an established fact.

## 3\. Current Workflow (Assumption)

The following workflow is assumed and must be verified through customer interviews:

1. A prospective student enquires about a course.
2. A staff member responds to the enquiry.
3. The staff member answers questions about courses, fees, batches, or schedules.
4. If the student does not book immediately, the staff member may need to follow up manually.
5. Some enquiries may be forgotten or abandoned before a booking is completed.

## 4\. Why This Problem Might Matter

The following are potential consequences to validate:

- Missed follow-ups may result in lost enrolments.
- Staff may spend substantial time answering repetitive questions.
- Enquiry management may become difficult during peak admission periods.
- Owners may lack visibility into enquiry-to-booking conversion rates.

These are possible impacts, not observed facts.

## 5\. Proposed Opportunity

An AI Sales Agent could respond to routine enquiries, collect relevant information, answer approved questions, schedule follow-ups, and help prospective students book a course or consultation.

This is a proposed solution, not a validated product decision. Its value and feasibility remain unproven.

## 6\. Assumptions to Validate

- Institutes receive enough enquiries for follow-up management to be a meaningful problem.
- Delayed responses or missed follow-ups occur regularly.
- These issues contribute to lost bookings or wasted staff time.
- Owners are willing to adopt an automated workflow.
- Automation can be implemented without compromising trust, accuracy, or customer experience.

## 7\. Evidence That Would Disprove the Hypothesis

The hypothesis would be weakened or disproved if interviews show that:

- Most enquiries already receive timely, consistent follow-ups.
- Missed follow-ups rarely affect bookings.
- The main reasons prospects do not book are price, course fit, or other factors that automation cannot address.
- Existing tools solve the problem adequately at an acceptable cost.
- Owners do not consider the problem important enough to change their workflow or pay for a solution.

## 8\. Validation Plan

Interview 5–10 representatives of coaching institutes or training centres.

### Participant-Selection Criteria

Select participants who:

- Work at or make operational decisions for a coaching institute or training centre.
- Have direct responsibility for admissions, sales, enquiry handling, or student counselling.
- Have personally handled or supervised prospective-student enquiries within the previous three months.
- Represent a range of institute sizes, course types, enquiry volumes, and operating models.
- Use different combinations of WhatsApp, phone, website forms, social media, spreadsheets, CRMs, or other tools.
- Include both owners or senior decision-makers and frontline staff, where possible.

Avoid relying only on referrals from one institute, participants who have no direct knowledge of the workflow, or people who have been shown the proposed solution before the discovery interview.

### Interview Method

Conduct 30–45 minute semi-structured interviews. Ask for recent, specific examples rather than general opinions. Do not pitch the AI Sales Agent during discovery interviews, and do not describe the hypothesis in a way that encourages agreement.

Use questions such as:

1. How do new student enquiries currently arrive?
2. Please walk me through the most recent enquiry you handled, from first contact to booking or abandonment.
3. Who is responsible for responding to each channel?
4. How quickly do you usually respond during normal periods and peak admission periods?
5. What happens when a prospect does not book during the first conversation?
6. How are follow-ups scheduled, assigned, and recorded?
7. Can you describe a recent enquiry that was followed up late, inconsistently, or not at all?
8. What happened to that prospect, if known?
9. How often do enquiries remain unresolved or become difficult to track?
10. Which questions do staff answer repeatedly?
11. How much staff time is spent responding to enquiries and following up?
12. How do you distinguish a lost enquiry from a prospect who is still considering the course?
13. What information do you use to measure enquiry-to-booking conversion?
14. Which tools are used today, and what limitations do they have?
15. What are the main reasons prospects do not book?
16. Which parts of the process require human judgement or personal trust?
17. What risks would make you unwilling to automate any part of the process?
18. What, if anything, have you already tried to improve enquiry handling?
19. What did that change cost, and what result did it produce?
20. If this problem disappeared, what measurable business or operational improvement would you expect?

Where appropriate, ask for supporting artefacts such as anonymised enquiry logs, follow-up lists, response-time reports, booking records, or screenshots of the current workflow. Do not request personally identifiable student information.

### Evidence Thresholds

Classify each interview statement as reported behaviour, observed evidence, estimate, opinion, or assumption. Give greater weight to recent, specific examples and records than to general claims or hypothetical interest.

Treat the hypothesis as more credible when:

- At least 5 of 8–10 participants independently report delayed, missed, or inconsistent follow-ups within the previous three months.
- At least 3 participants provide a specific recent example linking a follow-up failure to a delayed, lost, or uncertain booking, or provide records showing the pattern.
- At least 5 participants describe a recurring manual burden, such as checking multiple channels, maintaining reminders, or answering repetitive questions.
- At least 3 participants identify a measurable impact, such as staff hours, response-time deterioration, unresolved enquiries, or reduced conversion.
- The pattern appears across at least two institute sizes or operating contexts.
- At least 3 decision-makers rank the problem among their top operational or admissions priorities and express willingness to test a workflow improvement without being prompted by a product pitch.

Treat the hypothesis as weak or disproved when:

- Fewer than 3 of 8–10 participants report a recent follow-up problem.
- Most participants show that follow-ups are consistently handled through existing processes.
- Participants cannot connect missed follow-ups to meaningful operational or business impact.
- The dominant causes of non-booking are unrelated to follow-up and cannot reasonably be addressed by better enquiry handling.
- Existing tools are widely considered adequate, affordable, and consistently used.
- Decision-makers do not consider the problem important enough to change, test, or pay for a solution.

These thresholds are decision rules for the initial validation round, not proof that the problem exists or does not exist in the entire market.

### Evidence-Recording Method

Maintain an evidence log for every interview with the following fields:

- Participant ID, role, institute type, approximate size, and enquiry channels.
- Interview date and interviewer.
- Exact or closely paraphrased statement.
- Classification: supporting, contradictory, neutral, or unresolved.
- Evidence type: specific example, observed record, estimate, opinion, or assumption.
- Recency and context of the example.
- Claimed frequency.
- Operational impact.
- Business impact.
- Confidence level: high, medium, or low.
- Follow-up question or artefact requested.
- Whether the evidence is independent or may duplicate another participant’s account.

Keep a separate assumption-and-evidence table mapping each assumption to supporting evidence, contradictory evidence, and unresolved questions. Record contradictory evidence before summarising conclusions, and do not count multiple participants from the same institute as independent confirmations. At the end of the interview round, calculate the number and proportion of independent participants supporting or contradicting each assumption, review representative examples, and document any selection bias or missing evidence.

## 9\. Initial Success Criteria

The hypothesis becomes more credible if multiple independent interviewees describe recent examples of missed or delayed follow-ups, explain the operational or business impact, and show that their current process does not adequately solve the problem.

The validation round is complete when the evidence log contains interviews across the defined participant segments, each major assumption has supporting and contradictory evidence recorded, the stated thresholds have been assessed, and the team has documented whether to proceed, revise the hypothesis, or stop pursuing it.
